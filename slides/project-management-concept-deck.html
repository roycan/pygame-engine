The permanent situation

Every real software team lives what your squad lived in Project Safari: more good ideas than time, people, and skill. The menu will always total more than the budget.

So what are a team's choices — and which choice is project management?

Pick the answer you believe, then defend it in one sentence:

a) work harder and keep everything
b) cut scope deliberately and say what is excluded
c) promise everything and decide later
d) quietly drop features without telling anyone

a) "Work harder" — effort is real, but it is not a plan. The menu was 32; you had 12. Effort does not create tokens.
↓
c) and d) — promising everything, or silently dropping features, destroys the one thing a team runs on: trustworthy commitments.
↓
b) Cut deliberately — and say so. That is project management: organizing people, scope, time, resources, risks, and work so the team delivers something valuable — and can prove it planned to.

Meeting 1 · What is Project Management?

From project idea to coordinated, achievable work

The definition SG16

Project management transforms the SDLC phases into actionable steps. It keeps every stage aligned with users' needs, and it runs on four standing activities:

  • Plan — decide the scope, the order, and who leads
  • Monitor — watch the work against the plan (evidence, not memory)
  • Adapt — when reality disagrees, change the plan deliberately
  • Communicate — keep the squad, the client, and the teacher honest

Your Project Safari card was this

The MoSCoW marks were planning · the token check was monitoring · the Gamekeeper's food-patch ambush (Version C) forced adapting · the pitch sentence was communication. You have already done all four.

What PM is not

Not a guarantee of success · not bureaucracy for its own sake · not "one boss tells everyone what to do". It is systematic thinking, effective communication, and creative collaboration — SG 16's own words.

Meeting 1 · The Project Management Triangle

Three corners you juggle — one center you protect

How the corners talk: fix time and resources, and only scope can move — Project Safari's lesson. Demand more scope, and something must give: more time, more resources, or quality. Changing one corner always moves another.
The accuracy line: meeting deadline, budget, and scope does not automatically mean success — a project can hit all three corners and still be useless. Quality and value live at the center, and the center is what the corners must protect.

Meeting 1 · Prioritizing scope

MoSCoW — four marks that turn wishes into decisions

M Must have

The mission fails without it. In Project Safari: the chase rules — no chase, no predator–prey dynamics.

S Should have

Important and planned — you fund it if the budget allows.

C Could have

Nice, cheap, optional — funded only with leftover tokens.

W Won't have for now

Excluded — in writing, with a reason. The most professional mark on the list: it tells the client what to stop expecting.

The sentence that makes MoSCoW project management (not wish-listing): writing a feature into Won't is a deliberate, recorded decision — it tells the client what to stop expecting and protects the Musts from silent decay. P-1 Your exclusion lines were graded evidence, not apologies.

Meeting 1 · Two familiar project-management approaches

Waterfall and Agile — and the nine models behind them

Waterfall as PM approach

More upfront planning, largely sequential progression. The plan is signed, then the team walks the stages in order. Fits stable requirements and fixed contracts — SG 15's linear model, wearing a manager's hat.

Agile as PM approach

Shorter cycles, feedback, collaboration, adaptation. Grounded in values and principles (Agile Manifesto): individuals and interactions, working software, customer collaboration, responding to change. Agile is not "no planning" and not "coding quickly" — it plans constantly, in small increments.

How they relate to SG 15 — precisely: SG 15 taught nine SDLC models (Waterfall, V-Model, Agile, Iterative, Incremental, Spiral, Big Bang, DevOps, RUP). SG 16 highlights Waterfall and Agile because they are the two most familiar project-management approaches — but they are not the only ways to organize a project. The nine-model menu from SG 15 still stands, and many teams blend them (Agile itself is iterative and incremental).

Meeting 1 · Developing the proposal — the problem

Start with a problem, not with an application

The four words your proposal needs first

  • Problem — what is wrong, for whom, right now?
  • Context — where and when does the problem appear?
  • Need — why does it matter to someone?
  • Users & beneficiaries — who uses the app, and who gains even if they never touch it?
The honest test: if you cannot name a person whose situation improves, you do not have a problem — you have a wish. Wishes make toys; problems make projects.
"I want to make a game because games are cool."
↓
"New players of our starter game lose their hero without ever understanding the danger."
↓
Users: the players · Beneficiary: the Biology Department, whose lesson finally lands in-game.
·
Mission S-1's letter worked because it was a real problem, stated by a real stakeholder. Your proposal needs that same spine: a problem someone would pay (or grade) to solve.

Meeting 1 · Goals and SMART objectives

The dream — and the contract

Goal vs objective SG16 Annex B

Goal: the broad outcome you intend — the direction. Objectives: the specific, measurable results that prove you are walking that direction. Annex B asks for the goal paragraph followed by numbered objectives.

SMART: Specific · Measurable · Achievable · Relevant · Time-bound — the five questions a skeptical reviewer asks your objective.

WEAK: "Our ecosystem simulation will be good and teach players about nature."
↓ apply SMART
STRONG: "By the end of Milestone 2, the simulation runs 50 turns with the predator hunting enabled, and the live chart shows at least one full prey rise-and-fall cycle."
·
Why bother: the weak version can be claimed by anyone without building anything. The strong version either happened or didn't — and your squad can say which, with evidence. The 40-pt Project Plan rubric grades goals on exactly this SMART quality.

Meeting 1 · Proposed solution and project description

Annex B's big field — purpose, features, classes, users

# Project Safari — proposed classes (design, not code)

class Animal:            # shared body of every creature
    position (x, y) · energy · age
    move(direction) · act(view)

class Predator(Animal):  # IS-A Animal
    hunt()          # chase the nearest prey

class Prey(Animal):      # IS-A Animal
    flee() · reproduce()

class Ecosystem:
    grid · turn() · populations
    # places animals, advances days,
    # records the numbers the charts read

Every piece traces to a requirement, and every class names its attributes and methods — exactly what SG 16 Annex B's NOTE demands in the description.

The description must identify

  • System purpose — what the app is for
  • Features — what it does (your Musts and Shoulds)
  • System components — the classes, attributes, methods, and how they interact (the NOTE in Annex B)
  • Expected outputs — what appears: charts, boards, messages
  • Target users — named, not "everyone"
Three honest notes: these classes are proposed design elements — described in words, not a full class diagram, and not yet written. And they should feel familiar: act(view) on a grid is the pattern you have lived since your first RogueEdu villain. Old knowledge, new document.

Meeting 1 · Tools, libraries, and technical feasibility

Name your candidates — then earn them

Candidates worth knowing exist

  • matplotlib — charts and data visualization (Project Safari's population graphs)
  • pygame — interactive 2D graphics and game loops
  • NumPy — numerical computation on lists of numbers
  • Flask — web applications (RogueEdu runs on it)

None is mandatory; none fits every project. Naming a candidate that serves your requirement is the skill.

Four levels of honesty about a library

  1. 1 · It exists — you have heard the name
  2. 2 · You know its purpose — what problems it solves
  3. 3 · You can use it — you have made it do something
  4. 4 · You have evidence it works for YOUR project — a small experiment already ran
Be level-honest in the proposal: level 1–2 is allowed at proposal time. Levels 3–4 are earned during the project — and SG 17 (Frameworks and Libraries) is where levels 3–4 are taught properly. Claiming level 4 on day one is the Same Trap as the 99 that lied.

Meeting 1 · The Shinobi Skill Scroll — self-regulated learning

A competency with your name on it — plan it like a mission

The five lines every shinobi writes

  • What I already know — honest starting point
  • What I need to learn — the gap, named precisely
  • How I will learn it — the strategy
  • Which resource — named, findable, real
  • Evidence of competence — an artifact someone can check

SG 16 calls this self-regulated learning — a named desired competency. Your P-1 card already had one line of it; the proposal makes it official.

Need: "draw the population chart" — matplotlib
↓
Strategy: read the official pyplot tutorial, then follow one beginner example end-to-end.
↓
Practice: reproduce the tutorial chart with my own small data set — not by copying the tutorial's numbers.
↓
Evidence: a working chart of MY ecosystem data, with two sentences explaining what it shows.
·
The trap: watching a tutorial is not mastery — it is watching. The evidence line is what separates learning from watching.

Meeting 1 · Project risks and mitigation

Naming trouble early is a project-management superpower

RiskPossible impactMitigation
Dataset unavailableDevelopment delayedIdentify an alternative dataset early
Feature too difficultMilestone delayedBuild a smaller prototype first
Team member unavailableWork distribution affectedDocument progress; share responsibilities
Library unfamiliarIntegration problemsRun a small feasibility experiment

A risk is not failure — it is a failure you met in advance. Mitigation = the plan you make while everything is still calm.

Rank risks like a shinobi

Two questions, honestly answered: How likely? (rare → almost certain) and how bad if it happens? (annoying → mission-ending). The ones that are likely AND bad get mitigations written this week — the rest go on the watch list.

From Project Safari: "breeding numbers might explode and crash the board — test limits in milestone 1." That line was a risk with a mitigation, written before any code existed. You have already done this.

Meeting 1 · Milestones and leadership

The relay — three milestones, three different leads

Task = one piece of work · Milestone = a dated checkpoint bundling tasks · Deliverable = what the milestone hands over · Team lead = coordinates and is accountable — not the only worker.

M1 · Planning & design

requirements, wireframe, class sketch
Lead: Shinobi A — coordinates, runs the consistency pass

deliverable: the blueprint set

M2 · Working prototype

core feature runs; population behavior visible
Lead: Shinobi B — coordinates builds and the chakra checks

deliverable: the playable prototype

M3 · Final demonstration

charts, evidence, the presentation
Lead: Shinobi C — coordinates testing and the demo

deliverable: the demo + evidence pack

The rules that make the relay fair SG16: at least three milestones per quarter · one lead per milestone · every member leads at least one milestone every quarter. The lead coordinates and answers for the deliverable — the squad does the work. Customize these milestones for YOUR project; the names here are an example, not a template.

ILA · Is your proposal ready?

The readiness checklist — against SG 16 Annex B

The template's own fields

  • Title, section, members, date, duration — filled, real names
  • Description — the need, the features, the classes with attributes and methods, the target users
  • Goal + numbered objectives — the goal paragraph, then objectives that survive the SMART questions
  • Timeline — at least three milestones, one lead each, every member leading one

The two supplementary fields squad-decided

Integration: which discipline, what it contributes. Learning plan: each shinobi's skill line from the Skill Scroll. The official template has no boxes for these — your teacher decides where they live. (Slide 26.)

The final read-aloud test

One squadmate reads the description slowly; the others listen as strangers: "Do we know what to build? Do we know who it is for? Do we know how we will prove it works?" Three yeses — or revise.

Meeting 2 · Presenting the proposal

The nine moves, in the Annex's own order

1 · title + overview → 2 · problem + justification → 3 · goal + objectives → 4 · target users → 5 · solution + technical design → 6 · cross-disciplinary integration → 7 · timeline + milestones → 8 · team roles + collaboration → 9 · resources + constraints

Presenter rules

  • Every member speaks — the oral rubric scores delivery per member
  • Follow the proposal's order — the presentation is the document, spoken
  • Claims stay tied to evidence — say what proves each claim

Nothing extra

The sequence above IS the requirement list. Adding slide shows, trailers, or features you did not propose wastes the minutes your timeline needs — and invites questions about things you haven't planned.

Meeting 2 · Defending your decisions

Questions are not attacks — they are free testing

Seven questions you should expect

  • Why did you choose this problem?
  • Who benefits — and how, specifically?
  • Why is the project feasible for your squad?
  • Why these features — and why not others?
  • What happens if a milestone is delayed?
  • What if a library doesn't work out?
  • Why is your solution better than a simpler one?

The answering pattern

Claim → evidence → consequence. "We cut the map editor (claim) because it cost 4 of our 12 tokens (evidence); keeping the chase rules protects the Biology lesson (consequence)." Every answer has three parts.

The patterns to refuse

Defensive answers ("it will work, trust us"), invisible answers ("we'll figure it out"), and blame ("the engine is limiting"). G-2 taught the alternative: the quoted WARN earned more respect than any confident claim.

Meeting 2 · Peer critique

Be each other's Arena-Master

Six questions a peer reviewer asks

  • Is the problem clear?
  • Are the objectives measurable?
  • Is the project feasible for one quarter?
  • Are the deliverables identifiable?
  • Are responsibilities and milestones realistic?
  • Does the interdisciplinary connection make sense?

Each question gets an answer with one quoted line from the reviewed proposal — evidence, like every review you have given.

You have trained for this G-2

In Mission G-2 you reviewed real diffs and rejected a lying claim with quoted evidence. Proposal critique is the same discipline — the diff is the document, the WARN is the vague objective, the stamp is your approval.

The reviewer's vow

Specific over general · aimed at the work, not the person · paired with a suggested improvement. "Make it better" is not feedback — "objective 2 has no time bound; add 'by week 4'" is.
