Phases 1–3 — predict before you classify

PREDICT Requirement — or design?

A · "The player must be warned before entering the Slime's attack range."

B · "Place an Elder near tile (7,7) whose dialogue warns about the bite."

C · "The warning must be seen before the first bite, and the chakra check must pass without warnings."

The D-4 test applies: would the sentence still make sense if we solved it with a wall instead of an NPC?

A — REQUIREMENT. Player-facing truth. No solution in sight: any piece that warns in time satisfies it.
↓
B — DESIGN (wearing a disguise). It has already chosen the piece, the place, and the words. Fine in §3 — a straitjacket in §2, because it kills every other solution before the squad thinks.
↓
C — REQUIREMENT + validation. A statement of what must be true, plus how we will know. The SRS holds both.
·
Why the wall matters: put design into requirements and every alternative dies unread. Put requirements into design and nobody knows what to test. D-4's Law 1, formalized.

The whole journey

Seven activities — one of them you've only just named

1 · Planning ✓ → 2 · Requirements Analysis ✓ → 3 · Design ✓ → 4 · Implementation → 5 · Testing → 6 · Deployment → 7 · Maintenance

✓ = performed for real in Mission D-4 · plain = ahead of you. SG 15's table lists six rows — its row 6 combines Deployment and Maintenance; they are two distinct activities, and the SG itself defers deeper study of testing, deployment, and maintenance to CS4.

What each is for — one line each

  • Planning: why, for whom, within what scope?
  • Requirements Analysis: what must the system do?
  • Design: how will it be organized and presented?
  • Implementation: building the solution

…and the far half

  • Testing: checking it satisfies the requirements
  • Deployment: making it available to its users
  • Maintenance: fixing, updating, improving — for as long as it lives
Two honest warnings: the SDLC supports quality — it does not guarantee bug-free software. And the phases are not universally rigid or one-directional: how strictly you walk them, and how often you loop back, depends on the model you choose — which is Meeting 2.

Meeting 2 · SDLC models

Same phases.
Different rhythms.

Different projects organize the same phases in different ways.

Recall your own debate D-4 Phase 4

"Finalize the entire blueprint before coding — or build one small piece, playtest it, and improve?" Your squad chose and justified. That choice was a model choice — before you knew the word.

The distinction that matters

Phases = what every project does (planning → … → maintenance). Models = how a project organizes those phases — how rigid, how often you loop back, when the client sees something. SG 15's own line: "how rigid or how often these phases are done or followed depends on the SDLC model used."

Model 1 of 9

Waterfall — walk the stairs, in order

What it is SG15

A linear, step-by-step approach: each phase must be completed before the next begins. Plan everything → requirements → design → build → test → deliver. Going back is expensive, so you try not to.

Fits when: requirements are clear and unlikely to change — SG 15's own quiz example: a school club-proposal system with stable rules. Also: fixed contracts, regulated domains.

You know this feeling D-4

"Finalize the entire blueprint before coding" — the first half of the Phase 4 debate is the Waterfall instinct: sign the blueprint, then build exactly it.

The honest risk: for an unpredictable project, late changes are costly and users wait months before saying "this isn't what we meant." SG 15's quiz asks exactly why Waterfall is risky there — now you can answer.
© 2026 Sir Roy · source: SG15 §1.2 nine-model summary + Annex E quiz items14 / 25
Model 2 of 9

V-Model — every step plans its own test

What it is SG15

Verification and Validation: extends Waterfall by pairing each development phase with a corresponding testing phase. While you design, you also write the tests that will check that design.

When it fits

Requirements are stable and quality must be demonstrable — the mistake you can catch before building. Honest note: like Waterfall, the V is rigid — it inherits the same "changes are expensive" risk.

© 2026 Sir Roy · source: SG15 §1.2 (V-Model, Verification and Validation)15 / 25
Model 3 of 9

Agile — small deliveries, constant conversation

What it is SG15

An iterative, flexible approach that delivers software in short cycles (sprints) with continuous customer feedback. Each sprint produces something usable; the client's reaction steers the next sprint.

Fits when: requirements are expected to change or reveal themselves — products with real users whose feedback matters more than the original plan.

Not a short Waterfall

"Do Waterfall, but in little chunks" is not Agile. Agile is a different posture: expect change instead of resisting it, talk to users constantly instead of freezing requirements, re-plan every cycle instead of defending a signed blueprint.

You know this feeling D-4

"Build one small piece, playtest it with players, improve" — the second half of the Phase 4 debate is the Agile instinct.

Models 4 and 5 of 9 — siblings, not twins

Iterative & Incremental — distinct, and often combined

Iterative SG15

Develop through repeated cycles, refining requirements and design with each iteration. You revisit your earlier decisions on purpose. Fits when requirements are not fully clear at the start.

Picture: the same drawing redrawn — each version truer.

Incremental SG15

Build the software in smaller, functional parts (increments) delivered over time — each increment is usable on its own. Picture: the game gets one new monster per week; every week it plays.

Distinct but combinable: a team can refine its understanding iteratively while shipping pieces incrementally — Agile itself blends both with feedback. Read your SG's model summary carefully: a line about "minimal planning, coding begins immediately" has drifted into the Iterative bullet there — that describes Big Bang (next-but-one), not Iterative. Flagged, not silently fixed.

Model 6 of 9

Spiral — every loop passes through risk

What it is SG15

Iterative development combined with explicit risk analysis in repeated cycles ("spirals"). Each loop: decide objectives → identify the risks → handle them (often a prototype) → build and evaluate → plan the next loop.

Fits when: the project is large, expensive, or uncertain enough that knowing your biggest risk matters more than moving fast — each spiral exists to shrink the scariest unknown first.

The spiral's signature

Waterfall asks "what's next?" Agile asks "what did the user say?" Spiral asks "what could sink us — and did we face it this loop?" That question is the model.

Village picture

A squad that builds a prototype of the slime AI first, because "players might find it boring" is the scariest unknown — tests it with real players, then commits — just ran one spiral.

© 2026 Sir Roy · source: SG15 §1.2 (Spiral: iterative + risk analysis) · risk-first emphasis per approved brief18 / 25
Model 7 of 9

Big Bang — code first, think maybe

What it is SG15

Minimal planning; development begins almost immediately, and the process is spontaneous and unstructured — named after the theory of the universe's abrupt start.

Where it genuinely fits: trying things out, experimentations, side projects, throwaway prototypes — small, low-stakes, one-person efforts where discovery is the point.

The honest risk SG15 quiz territory

No requirements to test against, no design to check, no plan to revert to. For a real, graded, multi-person product, Big Bang is how squads ship broken games and call it "iteration". It is a legitimate choice for small and disposable — and a disaster for anything you must defend.

The confession you already own

Every programmer has Big Banged something — a quick script, a throwaway idea. The skill is knowing when Big Bang is a choice and when it is an excuse. Mission D-4's answer: anything with a client and a grade gets a blueprint.

Model 8 of 9

DevOps — a culture that never stops shipping

What it is SG15

Integrates development and operations — the people who build and the people who run — emphasizing automation, continuous integration, and continuous delivery (CI/CD). Not a phase list: a working culture where "done" means "running for users", improved by tiny, frequent, automated releases.

You have touched its edge already: RogueEdu's Render deploy — push to main, the class arcade redeploys itself — is exactly the automation DevOps formalizes.

Not just a sequence of phases

You cannot "do DevOps" by walking phases in order. It is a standing collaboration between building and running — with automation doing the repetitive parts — that loops continuously instead of ending at "delivered".

When it fits

Products that are always live and always changing — web services, games with updates, anything where waiting a month between releases loses users. The village arcade updates in minutes, not terms.

Model 9 of 9

RUP — four named stages of a structured journey

What it is SG15

The Rational Unified Process: a structured framework combining iterative and incremental development across four phases — Inception → Elaboration → Construction → Transition.

Fits when: a team wants the flexibility of iteration inside a formal structure with clear stage gates — heavier than Agile, more forgiving than Waterfall.

Inception

"Is this idea worth it?" — scope, stakeholders, the go/no-go. ≈ your D-4 Planning box.

Elaboration

"What exactly are we building, and what's risky?" — requirements solidify, architecture is proven.

Construction

"Build it in increments" — the longest phase, iteratively.

Transition

"Put it in users' hands" — beta, deployment, training, handover.

Check yourself — five model traps

True or false?

1. "Agile is just Waterfall done in small pieces." → FALSE. Agile expects change and re-plans every sprint; Waterfall freezes early. Different posture, not different size.

2. "DevOps is a sequence of phases you follow." → FALSE. It is a culture and practice — building and operations joined by automation and continuous delivery.

3. "Iterative and Incremental are the same thing." → FALSE. Iterative refines through cycles; Incremental delivers usable parts. Distinct — and often combined.

4. "Big Bang is fine for our graded project." → RISKY. Legitimate for small, disposable experiments — a disaster when a client and a grade are waiting for a defensible result.

5. "Following an SDLC model guarantees bug-free software." → FALSE. The SDLC supports quality — structure, tracking, review — but bugs survive every model. Testing exists because no model is a force field.
